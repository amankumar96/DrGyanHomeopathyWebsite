## What are Warts?

Warts are small, rough skin growths caused by infection with the human papillomavirus (HPV). They are very common and most people will experience one at some point in their life. Warts can appear anywhere on the body but are most often seen on the hands, fingers, knees, and feet. A wart on the sole of the foot is called a verruca. Warts are generally harmless and often go away on their own over time, although this can take months or even a couple of years.

## Symptoms

Common features of warts include:

- A small, firm, raised growth with a rough, grainy surface
- Growths commonly found on the hands, fingers, knuckles, knees, or feet
- Tiny black dots under hardened skin, often seen in verrucas on the foot
- Mild discomfort or pain, particularly with verrucas that bear weight when walking
- Growths that may appear singly or in clusters

## Causes & Risk Factors

Warts are caused by strains of the human papillomavirus (HPV), which infects the outer layer of skin. The virus spreads through:

- Direct skin-to-skin contact with a wart
- Contact with contaminated surfaces, such as damp floors around swimming pools or showers
- Sharing towels, socks, or shoes with someone who has a wart or verruca

The risk of infection increases when skin is wet, damaged, or in prolonged contact with a contaminated surface. Children and people with weakened immune systems may be more prone to developing warts.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Avoid picking, scratching, or biting warts, as this can spread the virus to other areas
- Keep feet dry and change socks daily if you have a verruca
- Cover warts or verrucas with a waterproof plaster when swimming
- Avoid sharing towels, shoes, or socks with others
- Avoid walking barefoot in communal showers, changing rooms, or around swimming pools
- Wash your hands after touching a wart or applying treatment

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Warts are rarely a medical emergency, but see a doctor if you notice:

- A wart that is bleeding, very painful, or rapidly changing in appearance or colour
- Warts on the face or genitals, which should always be assessed by a doctor rather than self-treated
- Warts that keep coming back or spreading despite treatment
- Signs of skin infection around a wart, such as increasing redness, warmth, or pus
- A weakened immune system alongside new or multiple warts

These situations warrant a doctor's or specialist's assessment to confirm the diagnosis and ensure appropriate treatment.
