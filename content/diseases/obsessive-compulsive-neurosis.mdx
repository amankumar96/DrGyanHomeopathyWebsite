## What is Obsessive Compulsive Neurosis (OCD)?

Obsessive Compulsive Neurosis, more commonly known as Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD), is a mental health condition in which a person experiences recurring, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges (obsessions) that cause significant anxiety or distress. To try to relieve this distress or prevent a feared event, the person often feels driven to repeat certain behaviours or mental acts (compulsions), such as checking, washing, or counting. OCD can range from mild to severe, and in more significant cases it can take up many hours of the day and seriously interfere with work, relationships, and daily functioning.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of OCD include:

- Intrusive, unwanted thoughts about contamination, harm, symmetry, or other distressing themes
- Excessive worry about causing harm to oneself or others, even when there is no real risk
- Repetitive behaviours such as excessive handwashing, checking locks or appliances, or counting
- Mental rituals, such as repeating phrases or numbers silently to "neutralise" a thought
- A strong need for symmetry, order, or things being "just right"
- Significant time, sometimes hours a day, spent on obsessions or compulsions
- Avoidance of situations that trigger distressing thoughts
- Recognition, in many cases, that the thoughts or behaviours are excessive, alongside difficulty stopping them

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of OCD is not fully understood, but it is thought to result from a combination of factors, including:

- Genetic factors, as OCD can run in families
- Differences in brain structure and chemical signalling, particularly involving serotonin
- Life events, such as significant stress, trauma, or major life changes, which can trigger onset or worsening of symptoms
- Personality traits, such as a tendency toward high responsibility or perfectionism
- Certain infections, in rare cases in children, associated with sudden onset of symptoms

OCD can begin at any age, though it often starts in adolescence or early adulthood.

## How Homeopathy Approaches OCD

Homeopathy approaches a person with OCD by considering their individual thought patterns, emotional state, triggers, and overall life circumstances, aiming to build a broader picture of the person rather than treating "OCD" as a single fixed label. A homeopathic consultation typically explores the nature of the obsessive thoughts, associated anxiety, sleep, and general temperament.

Some individuals explore homeopathy as a complementary approach alongside professional mental health care, hoping it may support general emotional well-being. OCD is a recognised mental health condition that benefits from evidence-based psychological and, where appropriate, medical treatment, and homeopathy should not delay or replace this care.

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment for OCD typically involves psychological therapy, particularly cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) with a specific technique called exposure and response prevention (ERP), and in moderate to severe cases, medication such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) prescribed by a doctor or psychiatrist.

Some people choose to explore homeopathy as a complementary approach alongside professional mental health treatment, hoping it may support general well-being. It should never replace therapy or prescribed medication. Continue your current treatment and consult your doctor before making changes.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Learn about OCD to better understand the condition and reduce self-blame
- Practice stress-reduction techniques such as mindfulness or relaxation exercises
- Try to gradually resist compulsions in small steps when guided by a therapist, rather than avoiding triggers entirely
- Maintain a regular sleep schedule, as poor sleep can worsen anxiety
- Stay connected with supportive friends, family, or support groups
- Limit excessive reassurance-seeking from others, as it can reinforce compulsive patterns
- Keep a symptom diary to share with your therapist or doctor, noting triggers and patterns

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

OCD-related distress should never be minimised with general self-care advice alone. Seek urgent mental health support if you or someone you know experiences:

- Severe distress or anxiety that feels overwhelming or unmanageable
- Inability to function in daily life, work, or relationships because of obsessions or compulsions
- Thoughts of self-harm or suicide, or feeling hopeless about the future
- Compulsions that are causing physical harm, such as skin damage from excessive washing
- Rapid worsening of symptoms over a short period

If there is any risk of self-harm or suicide, contact emergency services, a crisis helpline, or go to the nearest emergency department immediately. Do not wait for a routine appointment in these situations.
