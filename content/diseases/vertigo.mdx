## What is Vertigo?

Vertigo is a symptom, not a disease in itself, describing a false sensation that you or your surroundings are spinning or moving when they are not. It most often arises from a problem in the inner ear, which helps control balance, though it can occasionally relate to the brain or its balance pathways. Episodes can last from a few seconds to several hours or longer, depending on the underlying cause, and may come with nausea or difficulty keeping steady.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms associated with vertigo include:

- A spinning or whirling sensation, even while standing or sitting still
- Loss of balance or unsteadiness
- Nausea or vomiting
- Sweating
- A feeling that the surroundings are moving or tilting
- Jerky eye movements (nystagmus) in some cases
- Ringing in the ears or hearing changes, depending on the cause

Symptoms may be triggered or worsened by changes in head position, such as rolling over in bed or looking upward.

## Causes & Risk Factors

Vertigo usually stems from a problem affecting the balance organs in the inner ear or the nerve pathways connecting them to the brain. Common causes include:

- Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), caused by displaced calcium crystals in the inner ear
- Labyrinthitis or vestibular neuritis, inner ear infections or inflammation
- Ménière's disease, a condition affecting fluid balance in the inner ear
- Migraine-associated vertigo
- Certain medications
- Less commonly, problems affecting the brain or its blood supply

Risk factors can include increasing age, a history of ear infections or head injury, and certain migraine patterns.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Move slowly when changing position, such as getting out of bed or standing up
- Sit or lie down immediately when you feel dizzy to reduce the risk of falling
- Avoid sudden head movements during an episode
- Stay well hydrated, as dehydration can worsen dizziness
- Get adequate sleep and manage stress, as fatigue can be a trigger for some people
- Avoid driving or operating machinery during or soon after an episode
- Keep a diary of episodes, triggers, and accompanying symptoms to share with your doctor

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek urgent medical assessment if vertigo is sudden and severe and comes with any of the following:

- Double vision or other visual disturbances
- Slurred speech or difficulty finding words
- Weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg
- Difficulty walking or sudden loss of coordination
- A severe headache unlike any you have had before

These combinations of symptoms need prompt evaluation to rule out a stroke or other serious cause, rather than being assumed to be a benign inner ear problem. You should also see a doctor if vertigo is persistent, recurrent, or accompanied by hearing loss or ringing in the ears.
