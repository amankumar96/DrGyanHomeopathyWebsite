## What is Multiple Myeloma?

Multiple myeloma is a cancer that develops in plasma cells, a type of white blood cell normally found in the bone marrow that helps the body fight infection. In multiple myeloma, abnormal plasma cells multiply out of control in the bone marrow, crowding out healthy blood cells and producing abnormal proteins that can damage bones, kidneys, and the immune system. It is a serious blood cancer that requires diagnosis, staging, and ongoing treatment by a hematologist-oncologist. There is currently no cure, but with modern treatment many people experience long periods of disease control, and some very early or slow-growing cases may initially be monitored closely rather than treated immediately. This page is for general patient education only and does not replace specialist medical advice.

## Symptoms

Some people with multiple myeloma have no symptoms at first, and it is sometimes discovered through a blood test done for another reason. When symptoms occur, they may include:

- Bone pain, often in the back, hips, shoulders, or ribs
- Unexplained tiredness or weakness
- Frequent or recurring infections
- Easy bruising or unusual bleeding
- Shortness of breath
- Unusual thirst with more frequent urination
- Headaches, confusion, or stomach discomfort, which can be linked to high blood calcium
- Unintentional weight loss
- Kidney problems

Because these symptoms can resemble other conditions, proper diagnosis by a doctor, including blood and bone marrow tests, is essential.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of multiple myeloma is usually not known. Recognised risk factors include:

- Older age, with most people diagnosed at 65 or older
- Being male, as men are slightly more likely to develop multiple myeloma than women
- A family history of multiple myeloma
- A precursor condition called monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), or a solitary plasmacytoma
- Weakened immunity, including from HIV or immunosuppressant medication after a transplant
- Prior radiation therapy for another condition
- Workplace or environmental exposure to certain chemicals, such as pesticides, herbicides, or benzene
- Higher body weight

Having one or more risk factors does not mean a person will develop multiple myeloma, and many people with the disease have no identifiable risk factor.

## How Homeopathy Approaches Multiple Myeloma

Some patients living with multiple myeloma explore homeopathy purely as a general wellbeing and symptom-coping support alongside their prescribed oncology treatment, considering their overall constitution, energy levels, sleep, and how they are coping emotionally with a serious diagnosis and its treatment. A homeopathic consultation, if pursued, would typically focus on general wellbeing rather than the cancer itself.

It is essential to understand that homeopathy cannot diagnose, cure, treat, shrink, or slow multiple myeloma in any way, and there is no scientific evidence that it affects the course of this cancer. Bodies such as the NHS state there is no robust evidence that homeopathy is effective for any health condition. Multiple myeloma requires ongoing specialist hematology-oncology care, and delaying or replacing conventional treatment with homeopathy or any other complementary approach is dangerous and can be life-threatening.

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment for multiple myeloma is directed by a hematologist-oncologist and depends on factors such as the stage of disease, test results, symptoms, and overall health. Approaches may include close monitoring without immediate treatment for early or smoldering disease, medicines to control the cancer and prevent complications such as bone fractures and kidney damage, radiation therapy to ease bone pain or address a tumour pressing on the spinal cord, and in suitable patients, a stem cell transplant. There is currently no cure, but treatment can often bring the disease into remission and help manage symptoms over extended periods.

Some patients choose to explore homeopathy only as a complementary, general wellbeing approach alongside this essential medical care, never as a substitute for it. Continue your current treatment and consult your doctor before making changes.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Attend all scheduled appointments, blood tests, and scans with your hematology-oncology team, even when feeling well
- Take all prescribed medications exactly as directed, without stopping or skipping doses
- Report new or worsening bone pain to your care team promptly, as it may need evaluation
- Eat a balanced diet and stay as physically active as your care team advises, to help maintain strength
- Take precautions against infection, such as good hand hygiene, as treatment can weaken the immune system
- Stay well hydrated as advised by your doctor to support kidney health
- Seek support for emotional wellbeing, through counselling or patient support groups, alongside medical care

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Multiple myeloma requires ongoing specialist monitoring, but some symptoms need prompt or emergency medical attention:

- New or worsening bone pain, or pain following a minor injury that could indicate a fracture
- Severe or worsening fatigue, paleness, or breathlessness, which may indicate anemia
- Recurrent or severe infections, or fever
- Unexplained bruising or unusual bleeding
- Reduced urination, swelling, or other signs of kidney problems
- Persistent nausea, confusion, or excessive thirst, which can be linked to high blood calcium

Seek emergency medical care immediately for sudden numbness or weakness in the arms or legs, inability to walk or stand, sudden confusion, or loss of bladder or bowel control, as these can signal spinal cord compression, which requires urgent treatment. Do not drive yourself; call for emergency help.
