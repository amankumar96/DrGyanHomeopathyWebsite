## What is Psoriasis?

Psoriasis is a common, long-term skin condition in which the skin produces new cells much faster than usual, leading to patches of dry, flaky skin covered in scales. On lighter skin these patches often appear pink or red with silvery-white scales, while on brown or black skin they may look purplish, grey, or darker than the surrounding skin. Psoriasis tends to follow a pattern of flare-ups (when symptoms worsen) and periods of remission (when skin improves), and its severity varies widely from person to person. It is a chronic condition, meaning it is currently not curable, but it can often be managed over time.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of psoriasis include:

- Patches of dry, thickened skin covered with silvery-white or grey scales
- Pink, red, purple, or grey discoloured patches, depending on skin tone
- Itching, soreness, or a burning sensation around patches
- Small, drop-shaped sores on the chest, arms, legs, and scalp (a pattern called guttate psoriasis)
- Pus-filled blisters, more common in a rarer form called pustular psoriasis
- Pitting, dents, or separation of the nails from the nail bed
- Flaky scalp patches that can resemble severe dandruff

Patches most often appear on the elbows, knees, scalp, and lower back, but psoriasis can affect any part of the skin.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of psoriasis is not fully understood, but it is believed to occur when the immune system mistakenly speeds up the growth cycle of skin cells. Genetics plays a role — people with a family history of psoriasis are more likely to develop it.

Several factors are known to trigger or worsen flare-ups, including:

- Stress
- Smoking and drinking alcohol
- Being overweight or living with obesity
- Certain infections, such as streptococcal throat infections
- Certain medicines
- Skin injuries such as cuts, scrapes, or sunburn
- Cold, dry weather
- Hormonal changes, including during pregnancy

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment for psoriasis may include emollients (moisturisers), topical steroid or vitamin D-based creams, coal tar preparations, light therapy (phototherapy), and in more severe cases, systemic or biologic medicines prescribed by a dermatologist. These treatments are supported by clinical evidence and are typically the first line of care.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Keep skin well moisturised with regular use of emollients to reduce dryness and irritation
- Avoid scratching or picking at patches, as skin injury can trigger new flares
- Limit alcohol intake and avoid smoking, both of which are linked to worse symptoms
- Try to manage stress through relaxation techniques, adequate sleep, or activities you enjoy
- Maintain a healthy body weight through balanced diet and regular activity
- Protect skin from sunburn, while sensible, brief sun exposure may help some people
- Use mild, fragrance-free soaps and avoid very hot showers, which can dry the skin further

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek medical attention promptly if you notice:

- Rapidly spreading redness covering most of the body, with skin that feels hot, tight, or painful (possible signs of erythrodermic psoriasis)
- Widespread pus-filled blisters, especially with fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell (possible signs of pustular psoriasis)
- Signs of skin infection, such as increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or pus
- New joint pain, stiffness, or swelling, which may indicate psoriatic arthritis
- Fever or a rapid heartbeat alongside a sudden worsening of skin symptoms

These situations can require urgent medical care, so contact your doctor or emergency services without delay.
