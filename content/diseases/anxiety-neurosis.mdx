## What is Anxiety Neurosis?

Anxiety neurosis is an older term still used in some settings to describe what is now more commonly called generalised anxiety disorder, a condition marked by persistent, excessive, and often hard-to-control worry about everyday matters. Unlike the ordinary anxiety most people feel before a stressful event, this worry tends to be ongoing, affects multiple areas of life, and is often accompanied by physical symptoms of tension. It can begin at any age and ranges from mild to significantly disabling, but it is treatable with appropriate support.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of anxiety neurosis include:

- Persistent, excessive worry that is difficult to control, often about everyday things
- Restlessness or feeling on edge
- Muscle tension, headaches, or unexplained aches
- Fatigue, despite adequate rest
- Difficulty concentrating or mind "going blank"
- Irritability
- Sleep difficulties, such as trouble falling or staying asleep
- Physical symptoms such as a racing heart, sweating, trembling, or stomach upset
- Avoidance of situations that trigger worry

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of anxiety neurosis is not fully understood, but it is thought to arise from a combination of genetic, biological, psychological, and environmental factors.

Known contributing factors include:

- A family history of anxiety or other mental health conditions
- Ongoing life stress, such as work, financial, or relationship pressures
- A history of trauma or significant adverse life events
- Certain personality traits, such as a tendency toward perfectionism
- Other mental health conditions, such as depression
- Physical health conditions or substances, including excess caffeine, certain medicines, or thyroid problems, which can mimic or worsen anxiety

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Practise regular relaxation techniques, such as deep breathing, meditation, or mindfulness
- Keep a consistent sleep schedule and prioritise good sleep habits
- Limit caffeine, alcohol, and stimulant intake, which can worsen anxiety symptoms
- Engage in regular physical activity, which can help reduce overall anxiety levels
- Break tasks into smaller, manageable steps when feeling overwhelmed
- Stay connected with supportive friends, family, or support groups
- Consider writing down worries to help identify patterns and triggers

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek medical attention promptly if you notice:

- Thoughts of harming yourself or others, or feelings of hopelessness
- Panic attacks that are frequent, severe, or accompanied by chest pain or difficulty breathing
- Anxiety that is severely interfering with daily functioning, work, or relationships
- Physical symptoms that could suggest another medical cause, such as a racing heart, unexplained weight loss, or persistent palpitations
- Increasing reliance on alcohol or other substances to cope

These situations can require urgent medical or mental health support, so contact your doctor or a crisis service without delay.
