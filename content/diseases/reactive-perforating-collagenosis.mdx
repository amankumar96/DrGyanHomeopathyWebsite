## What is Reactive Perforating Collagenosis?

Reactive perforating collagenosis (RPC) is a rare skin condition that belongs to a group of disorders called perforating dermatoses. In RPC, altered collagen, a structural protein normally found deep in the skin, works its way up through the skin and is eliminated through the surface, producing visible bumps and crusted lesions. There is an inherited form, which usually appears in childhood and is linked to a collagen gene abnormality, and a more common acquired form, which tends to appear later in life and is closely associated with underlying conditions such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease. Because it is uncommon and can resemble other skin conditions, a confirmed diagnosis, sometimes supported by a skin biopsy, is important, along with checking for any underlying health conditions.

## Symptoms

Common features of reactive perforating collagenosis include:

- Red-brown papules (small raised bumps) and nodules, often with a central crust or keratotic (hardened, scaly) plug
- Lesions typically small, usually up to around 10 millimetres across, sometimes with a slightly raised or "umbilicated" (dimpled) centre
- A red halo or border may surround the lesions
- Intense itching, which is a prominent feature for many patients
- Lesions most often appearing on the extensor surfaces of the arms and legs, though they can occur elsewhere
- Lesions that may appear in clusters or in a linear pattern where the skin has been scratched

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact mechanism behind reactive perforating collagenosis is not fully understood. In the acquired form, which is more common, several contributing factors have been proposed:

- Diabetes mellitus, particularly when poorly controlled, is one of the strongest associations
- Chronic kidney disease, including patients on dialysis
- Microvascular insufficiency (poor blood flow in small blood vessels), which is thought to play a role, especially in people with diabetes or kidney disease
- Abnormally high levels of fibronectin (a protein involved in tissue repair) in the blood
- Abnormal processing (glycosylation) of collagen fibres, which may make them more likely to be eliminated through the skin
- Cold weather and skin trauma, including scratching, which can trigger or worsen lesions

The acquired form develops more often in women than men, and typically appears in adulthood. An inherited form exists separately, linked to a collagen gene abnormality and usually appearing in childhood, without the same links to diabetes or kidney disease.

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment of reactive perforating collagenosis generally involves two parts: treating the skin lesions themselves, and identifying and managing any underlying condition such as diabetes or chronic kidney disease. Skin-directed treatment may include topical steroids, keratolytic (scale-softening) agents, antihistamines to help control itching, and, in some cases, phototherapy. Good control of blood sugar in diabetes, and appropriate management of kidney disease, are considered important parts of overall care and may help improve the skin as well.

Some patients choose to use homeopathy as a complementary approach alongside this medical care, often seeking general skin comfort or well-being support. Homeopathy should not be used as a replacement for proper diagnosis or treatment of diabetes, kidney disease, or the skin lesions themselves. Continue your current treatment and consult your doctor before making changes.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Avoid scratching the lesions where possible, as trauma can trigger new lesions and increase the risk of scarring
- Keep skin well moisturised to reduce dryness and itching
- Protect skin from cold weather, as cold exposure has been reported to trigger or worsen lesions
- Keep fingernails short to minimise skin damage from scratching
- If you have diabetes, work with your doctor to keep blood sugar levels well controlled
- If you have kidney disease, follow your nephrologist's treatment plan closely
- Avoid self-diagnosing skin lesions that look unusual; seek a proper medical assessment instead

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Because reactive perforating collagenosis is rare and often associated with underlying diabetes or kidney disease, new or unexplained skin lesions matching this pattern, crusted, itchy, red-brown bumps, especially on the arms or legs, should prompt a medical evaluation rather than self-treatment. See a doctor promptly if:

- You notice new, unexplained crusted or dimpled skin bumps that don't resolve on their own
- You have not previously been diagnosed with diabetes or kidney disease and develop these lesions, since this presentation can be the first clue to an underlying condition that needs investigation
- You have known diabetes or kidney disease and notice these skin changes, as it may indicate your condition needs re-evaluation or better control
- Itching is severe, persistent, or significantly affecting your sleep or daily life
- Lesions show signs of infection, such as increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or pus

A dermatologist can confirm the diagnosis, sometimes with a skin biopsy, and coordinate with your doctor to check for and manage any underlying diabetes or kidney disease.
