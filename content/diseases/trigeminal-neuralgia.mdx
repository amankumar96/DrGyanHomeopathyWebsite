## What is Trigeminal Neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic pain condition affecting the trigeminal nerve, which carries sensation from the face to the brain. It causes sudden, severe, electric-shock-like pain in parts of the face, often on one side only. Episodes can last from a few seconds to a couple of minutes and may recur many times a day, or come and go in cycles with periods of remission. Because it is a long-term condition, ongoing medical management is usually needed.

## Symptoms

Common features of trigeminal neuralgia include:

- Sudden, severe, sharp, or shooting facial pain, often described as electric-shock-like
- Pain typically affecting one side of the face, around the cheek, jaw, teeth, gums, or lips
- Attacks lasting seconds to a couple of minutes, which may occur in clusters
- Pain triggered by light touch, chewing, talking, brushing teeth, cold air, or smiling
- Periods of remission between episodes, which can last weeks, months, or longer
- In some cases, a background dull ache alongside the sharp attacks

## Causes & Risk Factors

The most common cause is compression of the trigeminal nerve by a nearby blood vessel, which can wear away or damage the nerve's protective covering. Other possible causes and risk factors include:

- Multiple sclerosis, which can damage the nerve's protective sheath
- A tumour pressing on the nerve, in rare cases
- Injury to the trigeminal nerve, including from dental or sinus surgery
- Age, as the condition is more common in people over 50
- Being female, as trigeminal neuralgia is somewhat more common in women

In some people, no clear underlying cause is found despite investigation.

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment for trigeminal neuralgia usually begins with specific anticonvulsant medicines, such as carbamazepine, which are often effective at reducing the frequency and severity of attacks. If medicines do not adequately control symptoms or cause troublesome side effects, a neurologist may discuss other medical options or surgical procedures aimed at relieving pressure on the nerve or reducing its pain signals.

Some people look into homeopathy as a complementary approach to help them cope with symptoms alongside their prescribed treatment. Continue your current treatment and consult your doctor before making changes, particularly because stopping prescribed nerve pain medicines abruptly can cause symptoms to worsen.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Identify and, where possible, avoid personal triggers such as cold wind, certain foods, or specific facial movements
- Use lukewarm water and a soft toothbrush for oral care if touch is a trigger
- Try to eat on the less-affected side of the mouth, choosing softer foods during flares
- Keep a diary of attacks and triggers to share with your doctor
- Seek support for the emotional impact of chronic pain, as anxiety and low mood are common
- Discuss any changes in pain pattern promptly with your treating doctor

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek prompt medical attention if you experience:

- New, severe, or worsening facial pain that has not been assessed by a doctor
- Pain attacks becoming more frequent, more intense, or lasting longer than before
- Difficulty eating, drinking, or maintaining oral hygiene because of pain
- Numbness, weakness, or other new neurological symptoms affecting the face
- Facial pain accompanied by vision changes, difficulty speaking, or weakness elsewhere in the body, which could indicate a different urgent neurological problem

These situations warrant timely medical or neurological assessment rather than self-management alone.
