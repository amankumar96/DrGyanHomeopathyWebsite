## What is Chronic Fatigue Syndrome?

Chronic fatigue syndrome, also known as myalgic encephalomyelitis or ME/CFS, is a long-term condition characterised by persistent, often severe fatigue that is not explained by an underlying illness and does not improve with rest. It typically affects a person's ability to carry out everyday activities and is frequently accompanied by other symptoms such as problems with sleep, memory, and concentration. The exact cause is not fully understood, and the condition can vary considerably in severity between individuals.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of chronic fatigue syndrome include:

- Persistent fatigue that is not relieved by rest and is not due to excessive physical or mental exertion alone
- Post-exertional malaise, a worsening of symptoms after even minor physical or mental effort, sometimes delayed by a day or more
- Problems with sleep, including unrefreshing sleep or disturbed sleep patterns
- Difficulty with memory, concentration, or thinking clearly, sometimes described as "brain fog"
- Muscle or joint pain without obvious swelling
- Headaches of a new type, pattern, or severity
- Sore throat or tender lymph nodes in the neck or armpits
- Dizziness or worsening of symptoms on standing

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of chronic fatigue syndrome is not known, and it is likely that several factors are involved. Possible contributing factors include:

- A preceding viral or other infection
- Problems with the immune system
- Hormonal imbalances
- Genetic predisposition, as the condition can occur more often in some families
- Physical or emotional stress or trauma preceding onset in some cases

Chronic fatigue syndrome can affect people of any age, though it is diagnosed more often in adults, and more frequently in women than men.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Use gentle pacing of activities, balancing rest and activity to avoid triggering post-exertional malaise
- Keep a symptom and activity diary to help identify patterns and personal limits
- Maintain a consistent sleep routine as much as possible
- Eat regular, balanced meals to help support overall energy levels
- Avoid pushing through severe fatigue, as this can worsen symptoms afterwards
- Seek support from family, friends, or support groups, as living with a chronic, often invisible illness can be isolating
- Discuss any new exercise or activity plans with a healthcare professional before starting

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek medical attention promptly if you notice:

- Sudden, severe, or unexplained weight loss
- Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or fainting
- New neurological symptoms such as weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
- Thoughts of self-harm or a significant decline in mental health
- Fatigue accompanied by other worrying features such as unexplained fever, night sweats, or swollen lymph nodes that do not settle

These symptoms may indicate a separate or additional medical problem that needs urgent assessment, rather than being assumed to be part of chronic fatigue syndrome alone.
