## What is Vocal Nodules?

Vocal nodules are small, callus-like growths that form on both vocal cords, usually at the point where they come into the most contact during speaking or singing. They develop from repeated or excessive strain on the voice, similar to how a callus forms on skin from repeated friction. Vocal nodules are benign and not cancerous, but they can affect the quality and stamina of the voice, particularly in people who rely on their voice heavily for work or singing.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of vocal nodules include:

- Persistent or intermittent hoarseness
- A breathy, rough, or scratchy voice quality
- Voice fatigue, especially after extended talking or singing
- Reduced vocal range, particularly in singers
- A feeling of a lump or tightness in the throat
- Pain or discomfort extending from the throat to the ear in some cases
- Difficulty projecting the voice or a voice that "breaks" easily

Symptoms often worsen with continued voice use and may improve somewhat with rest.

## Causes & Risk Factors

Vocal nodules are usually caused by repeated strain or misuse of the voice over time. Common contributing factors include:

- Frequent shouting, loud talking, or singing without proper technique
- Occupations that demand heavy voice use, such as teaching, singing, or coaching
- Speaking at an unnaturally high or low pitch for extended periods
- Smoking, which can irritate the vocal cords
- Chronic throat clearing or coughing
- Acid reflux affecting the throat
- Upper respiratory infections that are not allowed to fully settle before resuming heavy voice use

Children who shout or talk loudly frequently can also develop vocal nodules.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Rest your voice when hoarse, and avoid shouting or whispering harshly, which can also strain the cords
- Stay well hydrated to keep the vocal cords lubricated
- Warm up your voice before heavy use, such as singing or public speaking
- Avoid smoking and exposure to secondhand smoke
- Use a microphone or amplification when speaking to large groups, where possible
- Manage acid reflux if present, as it can irritate the throat
- Seek guidance from a speech and language therapist on healthy voice techniques

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

See a doctor or ENT specialist if you notice:

- Hoarseness or voice change lasting more than two to three weeks, especially if you smoke, as this needs assessment to rule out other causes rather than being assumed to be simple nodules
- Difficulty breathing or swallowing alongside voice changes
- Coughing up blood
- A lump in the neck along with voice changes
- Unexplained weight loss alongside persistent hoarseness

These symptoms need prompt medical evaluation rather than being managed with voice rest alone.
