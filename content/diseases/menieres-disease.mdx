## What is Meniere's Disease?

Meniere's disease is a long-term condition of the inner ear that affects both balance and hearing. It is thought to relate to a build-up of fluid in the inner ear, which disrupts the normal signals used for balance and hearing. The condition typically causes episodes, sometimes called "attacks," that can last from twenty minutes to several hours, followed by periods where symptoms settle. Meniere's disease usually affects adults and most often involves one ear, though it can occasionally involve both over time.

## Symptoms

The main symptoms of Meniere's disease during an episode include:

- Vertigo, a spinning or whirling sensation, often severe enough to affect balance
- Hearing loss in the affected ear, which may come and go in early stages but can become more persistent over time
- Tinnitus, a ringing, buzzing, or roaring sound in the affected ear
- A feeling of fullness or pressure in the affected ear
- Nausea or vomiting during severe episodes
- Fatigue and unsteadiness after an episode has passed

Episodes can vary greatly in frequency, from several times a week to only occasionally, with quiet periods in between.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of Meniere's disease is not fully understood, but it is believed to be linked to abnormal fluid pressure and volume within the inner ear (sometimes called endolymphatic hydrops). Several factors may be associated with the condition or with triggering episodes, including:

- A family history of Meniere's disease in some cases
- Abnormalities in inner ear fluid drainage or absorption
- Autoimmune responses affecting the inner ear, in some individuals
- Viral infections, in some proposed explanations
- High salt intake, which may affect fluid balance
- Stress and fatigue, which some patients report as triggers
- Allergies, in some cases

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Reduce salt intake, as advised by your doctor, to help manage inner ear fluid balance
- Limit caffeine and alcohol, which some people find worsen symptoms
- Try to maintain regular, adequate sleep and manage stress levels
- Sit or lie down immediately when vertigo begins, to reduce the risk of falls
- Keep a diary of episodes and possible triggers to discuss with your doctor
- Avoid driving or operating machinery during or shortly after an episode

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek prompt medical attention if you experience:

- Sudden, severe vertigo with an inability to stand or walk safely
- Any new neurological symptoms alongside dizziness, such as weakness, slurred speech, double vision, or confusion, which need prompt review to rule out other causes
- A first-ever episode of severe vertigo, to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other conditions
- Sudden, significant hearing loss
- Vertigo following a head injury

These situations need medical review to rule out other, potentially more serious causes of vertigo, so do not assume they are simply a Meniere's episode without being assessed.
