## What is Steven Johnson Syndrome?

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome is a rare but serious and potentially life-threatening disorder that affects the skin and mucous membranes. It usually develops as a severe reaction to a medication, though it can occasionally follow an infection. The condition causes the skin and the linings of the mouth, eyes, and other mucous membranes to blister and peel away, similar to a severe burn. Because it can progress rapidly and affect large areas of the body, Stevens-Johnson Syndrome is considered a medical emergency that requires immediate hospitalisation, often in a specialised burns or intensive care unit.

## Symptoms

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome often begins with symptoms that can resemble a cold or flu, followed by more specific warning signs, including:

- Fever and a general feeling of being unwell
- A painful, spreading rash, often starting as red or purplish patches or target-shaped spots
- Blistering of the skin that spreads and leads to the top layer peeling away
- Painful sores or ulcers in the mouth, eyes, throat, genitals, or other mucous membranes
- Red, irritated, or swollen eyes
- Skin that feels painful to touch, even before visible blistering appears

Symptoms typically appear within days to a few weeks of starting a new medication and can worsen very quickly.

## Causes & Risk Factors

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome is most commonly triggered by a severe immune reaction to a medication. Medications that have been associated with this reaction include certain antibiotics, anti-seizure medications, and some pain relief or anti-inflammatory drugs, though many other medications have also been implicated. Less commonly, it can be triggered by an infection. Risk factors include:

- A personal or family history of Stevens-Johnson Syndrome or related severe drug reactions
- Certain genetic markers, which may increase sensitivity to specific medications
- Having a weakened immune system
- Recent use of a new medication, particularly within the preceding weeks

It is not caused by anything the affected person did, and it cannot be reliably predicted in advance for most people.

Suspected cases must never be managed with homeopathy or any home remedy, and doing so, or delaying emergency hospital care in favour of trying an alternative approach, can be dangerous and potentially fatal. ## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment for Stevens-Johnson Syndrome involves immediate hospitalisation, usually in a specialised burns unit or intensive care setting, where the trigger medication is stopped, supportive care is given for skin and fluid loss, and complications such as infection or eye damage are closely monitored and treated. Recovery can take weeks, and follow-up care, including with eye specialists, is often needed.

Homeopathy has no role in this emergency treatment and must never be used in place of, or to delay, hospital care. Continue your current medical treatment and consult your doctor before considering any additional therapy, even during recovery.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

During the acute phase, no self-care measure is appropriate — hospital treatment is required. During a supervised recovery phase, once discharged from hospital care, general measures may include:

- Following all wound and skin care instructions provided by your medical team
- Attending all follow-up appointments, including eye and skin checks
- Avoiding the suspected trigger medication, and any related medications, for life
- Informing every future healthcare provider about the reaction and the suspected medication
- Protecting healing skin from sun exposure and friction
- Seeking emotional support, as recovery from this condition can be physically and emotionally difficult

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Suspected Stevens-Johnson Syndrome is always a medical emergency. Call emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department immediately, do not wait for a routine doctor's appointment, if you notice:

- Widespread blistering or peeling of the skin
- Painful sores or ulcers in the mouth, eyes, genitals, or other mucous membranes
- A spreading, painful rash accompanied by fever, especially after starting a new medication
- Red, swollen, or painful eyes alongside a skin reaction
- Skin that is extremely painful to touch, even without visible changes yet

This condition can worsen rapidly, so any delay in seeking emergency care can be dangerous.
