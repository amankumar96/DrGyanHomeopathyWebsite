## What is Alopecia Areata?

Alopecia areata is a condition in which the immune system mistakenly attacks hair follicles, leading to hair loss that typically appears as one or more smooth, round patches on the scalp. It can also affect eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, or body hair. The patches are usually not scarred or inflamed, and the hair follicles themselves are not destroyed, which is why hair can often regrow over time. In some people, the condition progresses to total scalp hair loss (alopecia totalis) or loss of all scalp and body hair (alopecia universalis), though this is less common. The course of alopecia areata is unpredictable — it may resolve on its own, recur, or persist for a long time.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of alopecia areata include:

- Sudden appearance of one or more round or oval patches of hair loss, often around 1 to 4 centimetres across
- Patches most commonly on the scalp, but also possible on eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, or body hair
- Smooth, often slightly pale skin within the bald patch, without scarring
- Short hairs that narrow near the base, sometimes described as "exclamation point" hairs, near the edges of patches
- Mild itching, tingling, or a burning sensation before or during hair loss
- Nail changes, such as pitting or ridging, in some people

## Causes & Risk Factors

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune condition, meaning the immune system mistakenly attacks hair follicles and stops them from growing hair normally. The exact reason this happens in a particular person at a particular time is not fully understood, but several factors are associated with increased risk:

- A family history of alopecia areata, suggesting a genetic predisposition
- Personal or family history of other autoimmune conditions, such as thyroid disease, vitiligo, or type 1 diabetes
- Associated skin conditions, including eczema
- Significant physical or emotional stress, or illness, which some patients report around the time hair loss begins

Alopecia areata can occur at any age and affects people of all backgrounds.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Protect bald patches from sunburn with sunscreen, a hat, or scarf, as the skin may be more exposed
- Consider wigs, scarves, or hairstyles that help with confidence while hair regrows
- Try to manage stress through relaxation techniques, adequate sleep, or activities you enjoy
- Avoid harsh hair treatments, tight hairstyles, or excessive heat styling that could irritate the scalp
- Connect with support groups or counselling if hair loss is affecting your emotional well-being
- Keep a record of when patches appear or change, which can help your doctor track the condition

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Alopecia areata itself is not a medical emergency, but you should see a doctor promptly if you notice:

- Rapid, widespread hair loss affecting large areas of the scalp or body in a short time
- Hair loss accompanied by scalp redness, scaling, pain, or signs of infection
- Hair loss along with other new symptoms, such as unexplained fatigue, weight change, or skin changes, which could suggest an associated condition like thyroid disease
- Significant distress, anxiety, or low mood related to hair loss, which deserves support and attention in its own right

A doctor can confirm the diagnosis, rule out other causes of hair loss, and discuss suitable treatment options with you.
