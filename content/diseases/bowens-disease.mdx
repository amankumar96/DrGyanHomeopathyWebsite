## What is Bowen's Disease?

Bowen's disease is an early, non-invasive form of skin cancer, medically known as squamous cell carcinoma in situ. "In situ" means the abnormal cells are confined entirely to the outer layer of the skin (the epidermis) and have not grown into deeper tissue. It usually appears as a slow-growing, persistent red or pink scaly patch, most often on sun-exposed skin such as the lower legs, neck, or head.

Although it is an early and generally slow-growing condition, Bowen's disease is still a form of skin cancer and is not something to self-manage or ignore. If left untreated, a small proportion of cases can progress into invasive squamous cell skin cancer, which can grow into deeper tissue. Because of this, Bowen's disease always needs proper evaluation, diagnosis (often confirmed with a skin biopsy), and treatment by a qualified dermatologist or specialist. It is a manageable condition when identified and treated promptly.

## Symptoms

Bowen's disease typically develops slowly over months or years. Common features include:

- A patch of skin with clear edges that doesn't heal on its own
- A scaly or crusty texture, which may be flat or slightly raised
- Red or pink colouring on fair skin (it can be harder to notice on brown or black skin)
- A size ranging from a few millimetres to several centimetres across
- Occasional mild itching, though many patches cause no discomfort at all
- Appearance most often on exposed areas such as the lower legs, neck, head, or hands, though it can also occur in the groin area

Because it can resemble eczema, psoriasis, or a fungal infection, it is often misdiagnosed without a proper examination or biopsy. A patch that bleeds, ulcerates, or develops into a lump is a different matter and needs urgent review, as explained further below.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of Bowen's disease is not always clear, but several factors are strongly associated with its development:

- Long-term exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light from the sun or sunbeds, especially in people with fair skin
- Older age, as it most commonly affects people in their 60s and 70s
- A weakened immune system, such as from immunosuppressive medication after an organ transplant, or conditions like HIV
- Previous radiotherapy to the skin
- Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection
- Historical exposure to arsenic, in rare cases

Bowen's disease is not inherited and is not contagious; it cannot be passed to other people through contact.

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional management of Bowen's disease is determined by a dermatologist after confirming the diagnosis, often with a skin biopsy. Depending on factors like the size, location, and number of patches, treatment options may include cryotherapy (freezing with liquid nitrogen), topical creams such as imiquimod or 5-fluorouracil, curettage and cautery, photodynamic therapy, or surgical excision. In some cases where a patch is very slow-growing, a doctor may recommend careful monitoring instead of immediate treatment. Follow-up appointments are important to check that the area has healed and to watch for any recurrence or new patches.

Some patients choose to explore homeopathy only as a complementary, general wellbeing approach running alongside this medical care, never as a replacement for it. Continue your current treatment and consult your doctor before making changes.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Protect your skin from the sun with protective clothing, shade, and a broad-spectrum sunscreen of at least SPF 30
- Avoid sunbeds and minimise prolonged sun exposure, especially during peak UV hours
- Attend all appointments for diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up as advised by your dermatologist
- Keep the treated area clean and follow your specialist's aftercare instructions carefully
- Check your skin regularly for new or changing patches, and note any changes to show your doctor
- Maintain overall skin and general health through a balanced diet and avoiding smoking
- Discuss any complementary approach, including homeopathy, openly with your treating doctor before pursuing it

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Most Bowen's disease patches grow slowly, but certain changes need prompt medical review, as they can indicate progression toward invasive skin cancer:

- The patch starts to bleed or develops an open sore that doesn't heal
- It becomes raised, thickened, or develops a lump or nodule
- It grows noticeably larger or changes shape more quickly than before
- It becomes painful or tender when it was previously painless
- New patches appear elsewhere on the skin
- Any patch that was previously diagnosed changes in appearance after treatment

If you notice any of these signs, contact your dermatologist or doctor promptly rather than waiting for a routine follow-up appointment. Early review of any such change is important for timely diagnosis and treatment.
