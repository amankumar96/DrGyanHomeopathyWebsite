## What is Calcaneal Spur?

A calcaneal spur, commonly called a heel spur, is a bony growth that forms on the underside of the heel bone (calcaneus). It develops gradually, often as a result of long-term strain on the muscles and ligaments of the foot, particularly the plantar fascia, the band of tissue running along the sole of the foot. Calcaneal spurs are frequently found alongside plantar fasciitis, and while the spur itself is often painless, the associated inflammation of surrounding soft tissue is usually what causes discomfort.

## Symptoms

Common symptoms associated with a calcaneal spur include:

- Pain at the bottom of the heel, often described as sharp or stabbing
- Pain that is typically worst with the first steps in the morning or after periods of rest
- Discomfort that may ease somewhat with gentle activity but worsen again after prolonged standing or walking
- Tenderness when pressing on the underside of the heel
- Mild swelling at the front of the heel, in some cases

Some people with a calcaneal spur have no symptoms at all, and the spur is only noticed incidentally on an X-ray taken for another reason.

## Causes & Risk Factors

Calcaneal spurs form gradually due to repeated stress and strain on the heel bone and surrounding tissue. Common contributing factors include:

- Long-term strain from plantar fasciitis
- Being overweight, which increases load on the feet
- Wearing poorly fitting or unsupportive footwear
- Activities involving repetitive impact, such as running or long periods of standing
- Abnormal walking gait or foot mechanics, such as flat feet or high arches
- Age-related changes, as spurs become more common with increasing age
- Certain types of arthritis

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment for calcaneal spur and associated heel pain usually starts with rest, stretching exercises for the calf and plantar fascia, supportive footwear or orthotic inserts, and pain relief such as over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medicines. Physiotherapy, night splints, or steroid injections may be considered for persistent cases, with surgery reserved for rare, long-standing cases that do not respond to other measures.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Rest the foot and reduce high-impact activities during painful flares
- Stretch the calf muscles and plantar fascia regularly, especially in the morning
- Wear supportive, cushioned footwear with good arch support
- Use heel cushions or orthotic insoles if recommended
- Apply ice to the heel after activity to help reduce discomfort
- Maintain a healthy body weight to reduce strain on the feet
- Avoid walking barefoot on hard surfaces

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Most heel pain is not an emergency, but see a doctor promptly if you notice:

- Severe heel pain that prevents weight-bearing or walking
- Heel pain following an injury, with suspected fracture
- Signs of infection, such as redness, warmth, swelling, or fever
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the foot
- Heel pain that does not improve after several weeks of self-care and rest

These symptoms warrant a proper medical or podiatric assessment to rule out other causes and guide appropriate treatment.
