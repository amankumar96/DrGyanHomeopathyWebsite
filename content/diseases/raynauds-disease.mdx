## What is Raynaud's Disease?

Raynaud's disease, also called Raynaud's phenomenon, is a condition in which small blood vessels in the fingers and toes narrow excessively in response to cold or stress, temporarily reducing blood flow. This causes the affected areas to change colour, often from white to blue to red, along with numbness or discomfort. Episodes are usually temporary and resolve as the area warms, but they can be uncomfortable and, in some cases, signal an underlying health condition. Raynaud's can occur on its own (primary) or alongside another disease (secondary), with the secondary form generally being more significant.

## Symptoms

Typical symptoms of a Raynaud's episode include:

- Fingers or toes turning white, then bluish, then red as blood flow changes
- Numbness, tingling, or a cold sensation in the affected area
- Pain or throbbing as blood flow returns and the skin warms
- Episodes typically triggered by cold exposure, handling cold objects, or emotional stress
- In more severe or secondary cases, skin sores or ulcers on the fingertips

Episodes usually last minutes but can occasionally persist longer, and the pattern of colour change can vary between individuals.

## Causes & Risk Factors

In primary Raynaud's, the cause is not fully understood but relates to an exaggerated narrowing of small blood vessels in response to cold or stress. Secondary Raynaud's occurs alongside another condition that affects blood vessels, most commonly autoimmune and connective tissue diseases.

Recognised risk factors include:

- Being female, and younger age at onset, for primary Raynaud's
- A family history of Raynaud's
- Living in a colder climate
- Underlying conditions such as scleroderma, lupus, or rheumatoid arthritis, for secondary Raynaud's
- Smoking, which can worsen blood flow to the extremities
- Certain medications, including some blood pressure medicines and migraine treatments
- Repeated vibration exposure, such as from certain tools or occupations

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Dress warmly in layers, and wear gloves and warm socks, especially in cold weather
- Avoid sudden exposure to cold, such as reaching into a freezer without gloves
- Practise stress-reduction techniques, as emotional stress can trigger episodes
- Avoid smoking, as it narrows blood vessels further
- Limit caffeine, which may worsen symptoms in some people
- Warm hands gently with warm (not hot) water if an episode begins
- Exercise regularly to support overall circulation

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek prompt medical attention if you notice:

- Skin that remains blue, black, or very painful even after warming
- Sores, ulcers, or areas of skin breakdown on the fingers or toes
- Signs of infection such as increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or pus around an ulcer
- New Raynaud's symptoms appearing later in life, or alongside joint pain, skin changes, or other new symptoms, which may need assessment for an underlying autoimmune condition
- Raynaud's affecting only one side of the body, which should be evaluated

These situations need timely medical review rather than waiting to see if symptoms settle on their own.
