## What is Vitiligo?

Vitiligo is a long-term skin condition in which patches of skin lose their natural pigment, resulting in milky-white or pale patches that can appear anywhere on the body, including the face, hands, and areas around body openings. It happens when melanocytes, the cells responsible for producing skin pigment, are damaged or stop functioning, often due to the immune system mistakenly affecting them. Vitiligo is not contagious and is not a form of skin cancer, though it can have a noticeable impact on appearance and, for some people, emotional well-being.

## Symptoms

Common features of vitiligo include:

- Flat, white or pale patches of skin, often with well-defined edges
- Patches that may appear symmetrically on both sides of the body
- Premature whitening or greying of scalp, eyebrow, or eyelash hair
- Loss of colour inside the mouth or nose in some cases
- Patches that can slowly enlarge or spread over months to years
- Increased sensitivity to sunburn in affected areas due to reduced pigment

The extent and pattern of patches vary considerably between individuals.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of vitiligo is not fully understood, but it is believed to involve the immune system mistakenly targeting and damaging melanocytes. Several factors are associated with vitiligo, including:

- A family history of vitiligo or other autoimmune conditions
- Having an existing autoimmune condition, such as thyroid disease
- Significant physical or emotional stress
- Skin trauma, such as sunburn or cuts, in some cases triggering new patches (a phenomenon sometimes called Koebner response)
- Onset at any age, though it often begins before age 30

Vitiligo affects people of all skin tones, but the contrast may be more visible on darker skin.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Use sunscreen regularly on affected patches, as they are more prone to sunburn
- Consider cosmetic camouflage products if patches affect confidence, if desired
- Protect skin from cuts, friction, and sunburn where possible, as trauma can sometimes trigger new patches
- Seek support if vitiligo affects emotional well-being; counselling or support groups can help
- Maintain a generally balanced diet and healthy lifestyle to support overall health
- Keep track of any new or changing patches to discuss at follow-up appointments

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Vitiligo itself is not an emergency, and it is important to remember it is not contagious and not a form of skin cancer. However, you should see a doctor for evaluation if you notice:

- New patches that are spreading unusually quickly
- Unusual, irregular, or asymmetric patches that look different from typical vitiligo
- Patches associated with other symptoms, such as fatigue, weight changes, or symptoms suggesting thyroid or other autoimmune conditions
- Any skin changes you are unsure about, especially if they look different from your usual pattern

A doctor can confirm the diagnosis, check for associated conditions such as thyroid disease, and discuss appropriate management options with you.
