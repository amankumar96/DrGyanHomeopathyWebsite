## What is Hair Fall?

Hair fall, also called hair loss, refers to thinning or shedding of hair beyond what is typical for an individual. Everyone loses some hair daily as part of the natural growth cycle, with 50 to 100 hairs shed per day considered normal. Hair fall becomes a concern when it is noticeably increased, patchy, or progressive. It can be temporary, resolving once an underlying cause is addressed, or more permanent, as seen in hereditary pattern baldness. Hair fall can affect self-confidence, but in most cases it is not a sign of a serious underlying illness.

## Symptoms

Hair fall can present in different patterns, including:

- Gradual thinning on the top of the head or at the temples, common in pattern baldness
- Increased hair shedding noticed on pillows, in the shower drain, or on a hairbrush
- Patchy or circular areas of hair loss, which can occur in certain autoimmune-related conditions
- Sudden loosening of hair that comes out in handfuls, sometimes following illness, stress, or childbirth
- Overall thinning across the scalp rather than in a specific pattern

## Causes & Risk Factors

Hair fall can result from a wide range of causes, including:

- Hereditary pattern baldness, which runs in families and is the most common cause of long-term hair loss
- Significant physical or emotional stress, illness, or recent surgery
- Nutritional deficiencies, such as low iron or protein intake
- Thyroid disorders, diabetes, or other underlying medical conditions
- Certain medicines or treatments, including chemotherapy
- Hormonal changes, including pregnancy and childbirth
- Tight hairstyles or harsh hair treatments that pull on the hair over time

Conventional approaches to hair fall depend on the underlying cause. For hereditary pattern baldness, options may include medicines such as minoxidil or finasteride, though these need ongoing use and are not effective for everyone. Addressing an underlying cause, such as a thyroid disorder or iron deficiency, often helps resolve related hair fall. In some cases, doctors may discuss other options such as light treatment, scalp procedures, or wigs.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Eat a balanced diet with adequate protein, iron, and other nutrients that support hair health
- Avoid very tight hairstyles, such as tight ponytails or braids, that pull on the hair over time
- Be gentle when brushing or towel-drying wet hair, as it is more fragile when wet
- Limit excessive heat styling and harsh chemical treatments where possible
- Manage stress through adequate rest, relaxation, and healthy routines
- Avoid smoking, which has been linked to poorer hair and scalp health

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

See a doctor if you notice:

- Sudden, patchy, or rapidly progressive hair loss
- Hair loss accompanied by scalp redness, scaling, pain, or itching
- Hair loss alongside other symptoms such as fatigue, weight changes, or irregular periods, which may suggest a thyroid or hormonal condition
- Hair loss following a new medicine, illness, or significant stressful event
- Hair loss in a child, which should always be medically assessed

A doctor can help identify any underlying cause and discuss suitable options for your specific situation.
