## What is Migraine?

Migraine is a common neurological condition that causes recurrent attacks of headache, usually moderate to severe, often on one side of the head, and frequently accompanied by nausea and sensitivity to light or sound. Attacks can last from a few hours to several days and can significantly affect daily activities. Some people experience a preceding phase called aura, involving temporary visual or sensory disturbances, though many people with migraine never experience aura at all. The frequency of attacks varies widely, from occasional episodes to several times a month.

## Symptoms

Migraine attacks commonly involve:

- Moderate to severe headache, often throbbing and on one side of the head
- Nausea, and sometimes vomiting
- Increased sensitivity to light, sound, or smells
- Worsening of pain with physical activity
- Aura symptoms in some people, such as flashing lights, zigzag lines, blind spots, or tingling sensations, usually before the headache
- Fatigue, irritability, or difficulty concentrating before or after an attack

Symptoms and their intensity vary between individuals and even between attacks in the same person.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of migraine is not fully understood, but it is thought to involve changes in brain activity affecting nerve signals, chemicals, and blood vessels. Several factors are associated with migraine or can trigger attacks, including:

- A family history of migraine
- Hormonal changes, particularly in women, linked to the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, or menopause
- Stress or, sometimes, the relaxation period after stress
- Poor or irregular sleep patterns
- Certain foods, caffeine, or alcohol, in some individuals
- Skipping meals or dehydration
- Sensory triggers such as bright lights, strong smells, or loud noises
- Changes in weather or environment

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Keep a headache diary to help identify personal triggers and patterns
- Maintain regular sleep, meal, and hydration routines
- Manage stress through relaxation techniques, exercise, or activities you enjoy
- Limit or identify any dietary triggers such as caffeine, alcohol, or specific foods
- Rest in a quiet, darkened room during an attack if possible
- Consider regular, moderate exercise, which some people find helps reduce attack frequency over time

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek urgent medical assessment, rather than assuming it is "just a migraine," if you experience:

- A sudden, severe headache unlike anything you have had before, often described as a "thunderclap" headache
- Headache along with fever, a stiff neck, or a rash
- Headache following a head injury
- New neurological symptoms such as weakness, vision loss, confusion, difficulty speaking, or slurred speech
- A significant change in your usual headache pattern, or headaches that are increasing in frequency or severity

These features need prompt medical evaluation to rule out more serious underlying causes, and should not be self-managed as a typical migraine attack.
