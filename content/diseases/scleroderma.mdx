## What is Scleroderma?

Scleroderma is an uncommon, long-term autoimmune condition in which the immune system causes the body to produce too much collagen, leading to thickening and hardening of the skin. In some people it affects only the skin (localised scleroderma), while in others, a form called systemic sclerosis can also involve internal organs such as the lungs, heart, digestive system, and kidneys. The condition varies widely in severity between individuals, and while it is a lifelong condition, many people are able to manage symptoms with ongoing specialist care.

## Symptoms

Symptoms of scleroderma can vary depending on the type and extent of the condition, and may include:

- Patches or widespread areas of thickened, hardened, shiny skin
- Tightening of the skin on the fingers, hands, or face
- Raynaud's phenomenon, where fingers or toes turn white, blue, then red in response to cold or stress
- Joint pain and stiffness
- Difficulty swallowing or digestive symptoms such as heartburn
- Shortness of breath, in cases where the lungs are affected
- Fatigue

Not everyone experiences all of these symptoms, and the pattern can differ considerably from person to person.

## Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of scleroderma is not known, but it is understood to be an autoimmune condition, where the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own connective tissue, leading to excess collagen production. Possible contributing factors include:

- Genetic predisposition, though scleroderma is not typically directly inherited
- Immune system abnormalities
- Environmental exposures, such as certain chemicals or solvents, in some cases
- Being female, as scleroderma is more common in women, particularly between the ages of 30 and 50

Scleroderma is not caused by anything a person did or did not do, and there is no known way to prevent it with certainty.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Keep hands, feet, and the whole body warm, especially in cold weather, to reduce Raynaud's episodes
- Moisturise skin regularly to help with dryness and tightness
- Avoid smoking, as it can worsen blood flow problems
- Do gentle, regular exercise to help maintain joint and skin flexibility, as advised by your physiotherapist
- Eat smaller, more frequent meals if digestive symptoms such as reflux are present
- Attend all scheduled specialist follow-up appointments, even when feeling well

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek prompt medical attention if you notice:

- New or worsening shortness of breath, chest pain, or a persistent cough, which could indicate lung or heart involvement
- Severe or worsening Raynaud's episodes with skin ulcers, sores, or colour changes that do not resolve
- Significant difficulty swallowing or persistent vomiting
- Sudden swelling, severe headache, or very high blood pressure, which can occur in a serious complication called scleroderma renal crisis
- Signs of infection in skin ulcers, such as increasing redness, pus, or fever

These can be signs of serious complications and need urgent specialist assessment rather than routine follow-up.
