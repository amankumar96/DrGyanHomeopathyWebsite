## What is Callosities?

Callosities, which include corns and calluses, are areas of thickened, hardened skin that develop as the skin's natural protective response to repeated pressure or friction. Calluses tend to form over broader areas, such as the sole of the foot or the palm of the hand, while corns are smaller and more concentrated, often forming on or between toes where bone presses against skin. Although usually harmless, callosities can become uncomfortable or painful, particularly if they continue to thicken or if there is an underlying bony prominence or gait abnormality contributing to the pressure.

## Symptoms

Common features of callosities include:

- Thickened, rough, or hardened patches of skin
- Yellowish or greyish discolouration of the affected skin
- A corn may have a hard, waxy centre surrounded by inflamed skin, or appear softer if located between toes
- Tenderness or pain when pressure is applied to the area
- Flaky, dry skin surrounding the thickened patch
- Calluses are usually painless unless they become very thick or cracked

## Causes & Risk Factors

Callosities develop due to repeated friction or pressure on the skin. Common contributing factors include:

- Ill-fitting shoes that are too tight, too loose, or have high heels
- Not wearing socks, or wearing poorly fitting socks
- Repetitive activities that put pressure on the hands, such as manual work or playing certain instruments or sports
- Abnormal foot structure or gait, such as bunions, hammertoes, or flat feet
- Walking or standing for long periods
- Thin or ageing skin, which is more prone to friction injury

## Homeopathy and Conventional Treatment

Conventional management of callosities usually involves removing the source of friction or pressure, such as switching to better-fitting footwear or using protective padding, along with regular gentle removal of thickened skin using a pumice stone or moisturising with urea or salicylic acid-based creams. A podiatrist may safely trim thicker corns or calluses, particularly in people with diabetes or circulation problems who should avoid self-treatment.

Patients interested in homeopathy should discuss this with their doctor or podiatrist and consider it only as a complementary addition to, never a replacement for, these established foot care measures. Continue your current treatment and consult your doctor before making changes.

## Lifestyle & Self-care Tips

- Wear well-fitting shoes with enough room in the toe area and good cushioning
- Use protective padding or cushioned insoles over pressure points
- Soak the affected area in warm water to soften skin before gently filing with a pumice stone
- Moisturise regularly with a cream containing urea or salicylic acid, if suitable for your skin
- Wear seamless, well-fitting socks to reduce friction
- Avoid walking barefoot on hard or rough surfaces
- Address any underlying foot structure issues, such as bunions, with professional advice

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

See a doctor or podiatrist promptly, rather than self-treating, if you notice:

- Signs of infection, such as redness, warmth, swelling, pus, or increasing pain around a corn or callus
- A corn or callus that bleeds, cracks deeply, or does not heal
- You have diabetes, poor circulation, or nerve damage in the feet, as these conditions raise the risk of complications from minor skin injuries
- Significant pain that affects your ability to walk or carry out daily activities

These situations call for professional foot care rather than home treatment, as they can indicate a higher risk of skin breakdown or infection.
