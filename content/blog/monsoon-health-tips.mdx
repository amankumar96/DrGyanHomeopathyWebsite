Every year, the first good monsoon shower in Ghaziabad and the wider NCR brings welcome relief from the heat — and, close behind it, a predictable rise in certain health complaints at the clinic. Most of these are preventable with a few consistent habits at home. Here's a practical look at what tends to go wrong during the rainy season in North India, and what actually helps.

## Water-Borne Illness: The Biggest Monsoon Risk

Heavy rain often overwhelms drainage and water supply systems, increasing the chance that drinking water gets contaminated with sewage or runoff. This is the main reason cholera, typhoid, and hepatitis A and E cases tend to climb during the monsoon in many parts of India, along with simple bacterial or viral diarrhoea.

The single most effective thing you can do is treat your drinking water seriously during these months:

- Boil water for drinking and cooking, or use a reliable filter/purifier rated to remove bacteria and viruses, not just sediment.
- Avoid street-side beverages, cut fruit, and ice made with uncertain water sources.
- Wash fruits and vegetables with clean water before eating or cooking them.
- Wash hands with soap before eating and after using the toilet — a basic step, but one of the most protective against food poisoning, as the NHS notes in its guidance on preventing food poisoning.

If you or a family member develops persistent vomiting, watery diarrhoea, high fever, or jaundice (yellowing of the eyes or skin), please see a doctor promptly rather than waiting it out. Dehydration from diarrhoeal illness can escalate quickly, especially in young children and the elderly, and oral rehydration solution (ORS) plus timely medical assessment matters more than any particular remedy at that stage.

## Mosquito-Borne Fevers: Dengue, Malaria and Chikungunya

Stagnant water collecting in coolers, flowerpots, discarded containers, and clogged drains becomes a breeding ground for Aedes mosquitoes, which spread dengue and chikungunya, and Anopheles mosquitoes, which spread malaria. The World Health Organization's dengue prevention guidance centres on exactly this point: eliminating standing water is the most effective way to cut mosquito breeding, since it only takes a small amount of stagnant water for eggs to hatch.

At home, this means:

- Emptying, scrubbing, and drying coolers and flowerpot trays at least twice a week.
- Covering stored water containers and overhead tanks.
- Clearing blocked drains and gutters around the house.
- Using mosquito nets, screens, and repellents, especially around dawn and dusk.

Dengue in particular can range from a mild fever to a more serious illness. Warning signs that need urgent medical attention include high fever that doesn't settle, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding gums, or unusual bruising. These are not symptoms to manage at home — please get a blood test and medical evaluation without delay if they appear.

## Fungal Skin Infections

Prolonged dampness, wet clothing, and humidity make the monsoon prime season for fungal skin infections such as ringworm (tinea), athlete's foot, and fungal rashes in skin folds. The NHS describes ringworm as presenting with an itchy, scaly, often ring-shaped red or silvery rash, and notes that it is not actually caused by a worm but by a fungus that thrives in warm, moist conditions.

Simple prevention habits go a long way:

- Dry your skin thoroughly after getting wet, particularly between toes and in skin folds.
- Change out of damp clothes and socks as soon as possible.
- Avoid sharing towels, combs, or footwear.
- Keep skin folds ventilated where possible, and choose breathable fabrics.

Mild fungal rashes often respond to basic hygiene measures and over-the-counter antifungal treatment from a pharmacy. If a rash spreads, doesn't improve after a couple of weeks, or appears on the scalp, it's worth getting it looked at rather than guessing.

## Digestive Upset

Beyond frank food poisoning, many people notice general digestive sluggishness, bloating, or loose motions during the monsoon — often related to humidity affecting food storage, a higher temptation to eat fried street food, and water quality fluctuations. Favouring freshly cooked, warm, home food over raw salads and street snacks during this period is a reasonable, low-cost precaution.

## When Home Care Isn't Enough

As a homeopathic physician, I often see monsoon patients for general constitutional support, recurring minor complaints, and recovery support alongside conventional treatment. However, for any suspected water-borne or mosquito-borne infection — especially with high fever, dehydration, or jaundice — timely diagnosis and conventional medical care (fluids, antibiotics, or antivirals where indicated) should always come first. Please continue any medication your doctor has prescribed, and treat this article as general seasonal guidance, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

Stay dry, boil your water, keep stagnant water out of your surroundings, and don't hesitate to get checked if symptoms seem more than mild — that combination covers most of what the monsoon throws at us in this region.
