Every year around October and November, as temperatures drop and stubble burning combines with vehicle and construction dust and still winter air, the Delhi-NCR region — including Ghaziabad and Vaishali — sees a sharp rise in air pollution levels. Along with it comes a predictable wave of patients with coughing, throat irritation, watery eyes, and worsened breathing symptoms. Some of this is genuine seasonal allergy (allergic rhinitis), and a good deal of it is the air itself irritating otherwise healthy airways. It's worth understanding the difference, and what actually helps.

## Why This Season Is Different

Cold, still winter air traps pollutants closer to the ground instead of letting them disperse, which is part of why smog tends to thicken in November and December in North India. According to MedlinePlus, short-term exposure to higher levels of outdoor air pollution is linked to reduced lung function, asthma flare-ups, cardiac symptoms, and increased emergency visits and hospital admissions — effects that are well documented beyond just "a bit of coughing."

People with asthma, COPD, allergic rhinitis, or heart conditions are more vulnerable, but even healthy adults and children can notice throat irritation, eye irritation, and fatigue on heavy smog days.

## Common Symptoms to Watch For

Based on guidance from Asthma + Lung UK and general respiratory health literature, the symptoms that tend to flare with poor air quality include:

- Increased coughing, especially at night
- Wheezing or a feeling of tightness in the chest
- Sore or irritated throat, and a dry, tickly cough
- Watery, itchy, or burning eyes
- Breathlessness that's more noticeable during normal activity
- Needing a reliever inhaler more often, for those who have asthma

If you have a diagnosed respiratory condition and you find yourself reaching for your inhaler more frequently, that's a signal worth discussing with your treating doctor rather than simply managing in silence.

## Practical Self-Care for Smog Days

None of the following claims to "cure" pollution exposure — they are reasonable, evidence-informed ways to reduce the burden on your airways:

- **Check air quality before planning outdoor time.** Apps and government air quality index (AQI) data can tell you when levels are particularly poor, so you can shift outdoor exercise, school sports, or morning walks to a better window.
- **Avoid peak traffic hours for outdoor activity.** Pollution from vehicles tends to peak during morning and evening rush hours; walking on quieter roads away from heavy traffic reduces your direct exposure, a point Asthma + Lung UK specifically highlights in its guidance for high-pollution days.
- **Keep windows closed during the worst periods**, particularly if you live near a main road, and consider an air purifier with a HEPA filter for bedrooms, which can meaningfully reduce indoor particulate levels.
- **A well-fitting mask (N95/N99-type)** can reduce the amount of particulate matter you inhale outdoors, particularly useful for anyone with existing lung sensitivity.
- **Keep prescribed inhalers and allergy medication on hand** and continue them as directed by your physician — this is not the season to skip or taper asthma treatment on your own.
- **Stay hydrated and avoid smoking or exposure to tobacco smoke**, which compounds airway irritation on top of ambient pollution.

## When to See a Doctor

Please don't wait out the following:

- Breathlessness that interferes with daily activities or sleep
- Wheezing or chest tightness that doesn't ease with your usual reliever medication
- A cough lasting more than two to three weeks
- Symptoms in a young child, elderly family member, or anyone with known asthma, COPD, or heart disease that are worse than their usual baseline

Air pollution-related symptoms in someone with an existing respiratory or cardiac condition are a medical matter first. Conventional assessment — and, where indicated, adjustment of prescribed inhaled or oral medication by the treating doctor — should always take priority.

## Where Homeopathic Care Fits

At the clinic, I see many patients during this season for general constitutional support, recurrent colds, and management of chronic sensitivities such as allergic rhinitis, often alongside their existing conventional treatment rather than instead of it. Individualised homeopathic care can be a reasonable complement for some patients' overall wellbeing, but it is not a substitute for prescribed asthma or cardiac medication, and it does not change the air quality itself. The most protective thing anyone can do this winter is combine basic exposure-reduction habits with consistent use of whatever medication your doctor has already prescribed, and to seek medical review promptly if breathing symptoms escalate.
